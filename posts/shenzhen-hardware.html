Why a Shenzhen Team Can Test 500 Ideas a Year

Round trips, gongkai and the price of a question: an interactive reading of Huang's The Hardware Hacker

Jimmy Li

Notes on the Agent Era

6 November 2026 · v2

Abstract

Shenzhen's lead in hardware is usually explained by cost. Following Huang [1], this essay argues that its real advantage is the price of a question: the time and money it takes to ask a supplier something and hold the answer. I model product development as a network of suppliers (§2), define the round trip as its unit of progress (§3), and show how gongkai reference designs lower the cost of a first working unit (§4). Together these give a simple bound on how many ideas a team can put in front of users in a year. Under illustrative parameters the bound moves from about twenty to several hundred (§5). Since only users can say whether a product is wanted, the number of tries matters more than the speed of any single one. Every figure is interactive. All parameters are illustrative and listed in Appendix A.

Keywords: hardware innovation; industrial clusters; Shenzhen; gongkai; iteration cost.

1Introduction

Shenzhen's rise as the world's hardware workshop is usually explained by cost: cheap labour, cheap parts, cheap land. Andrew "bunnie" Huang, who spent years building products such as the chumby and the Novena laptop with Shenzhen factories, tells a different story in The Hardware Hacker [1]. In his account the parts market, the board fab, the mould shop and the assembly line are a short walk or taxi ride apart. The advantage, he argues, is less about what things cost than about how quickly a question gets answered.

This essay restates that argument as a small model. It makes three claims, each stated as a proposition and each paired with a figure you can operate:

  1. The unit of progress in hardware is the round trip to a supplier. In Shenzhen it takes hours rather than weeks (Proposition 1, §3).
  2. Market parts and gongkai reference designs make an idea cheap enough that a team can put hundreds a year in front of users (Proposition 2, §§4–5).
  3. Speed settles whether a product works and whether it can be made. Only users settle whether anyone wants it, so the number of tries is what matters (Proposition 3, §5.2).

Reading guide. Figures tagged interactive respond to input, and most evaluate their equation as you go. The numbers are illustrative, chosen to show the shape of the argument; Appendix A lists them. Hover over any cross-reference, such as (4), [3] or a footnote mark, to preview it.

2The supply network

Definition 1 (Supply network). The supply network of a product is a directed graph G = (V, E). Its vertices are stages: materials, components, fabrication, assembly, testing and delivery. An edge (u, v) ∈ E means stage v depends on the output of stage u. A feedback edge points upstream: a problem found at v that has to be fixed at an earlier stage u.

From outside, making a product looks like a conveyor belt. Up close it is the graph in Figure 1. Testing sends problems back to fabrication, a wrong part sends you back to the market, and procurement, engineering, logistics and quality touch every stage.1 Every edge is a conversation someone has to have. Feedback edges are the expensive ones, because they are discovered late and have to travel back upstream.

1A real product has hundreds of line items and dozens of suppliers. Six stages is the version you can hold in your head. ↩

3The round trip

Definition 2 (Round trip). A round trip is the time τ from asking a supplier a question to holding the answer: a part, a board or a measurement. A debugging loop of K steps has cycle time T, given by (1).
T = Σ τₖ (k = 1 … K)
(1)

Huang's Shenzhen is a place where you go and look [1]. You walk the floors of the Huaqiangbei (华强北) electronics markets and leave with parts the same afternoon. You stand on the factory floor while your board goes through the line. In practice the factory engineer is also a WeChat contact, messaged many times a day. None of this lowers a unit price. All of it shortens τ.

Proposition 1 (Round trip) C1. In Shenzhen the round trip to a supplier is measured in hours rather than weeks. This, more than unit price, is the source of its speed.

4Gongkai: starting from what already works

Definition 3 (Gongkai). Gongkai (公开, "open") is Huang's term for the Shenzhen practice of passing reference designs, schematics and modules between firms [1], [2]. It isn't open source in the licensed sense. The documents are simply shared, copied and built on.

For a builder, gongkai means you rarely start from a blank page. A chip vendor's reference board already works. A module maker sells radio parts that are already certified. A public mould (公模) gives you an enclosure that someone else has already paid to tool. If the blocks i = 1, …, n of a product are developed in parallel, the first working unit takes

T₀ = maxᵢ tᵢ + t_int
(2)

weeks and costs

C₀ = Σᵢ cᵢ + C_pilot
(3)

where tᵢ and cᵢ are the time and cost of block i, t_int is one week of integration, and C_pilot is a 50-unit pilot run.

The two equations behave differently, and Figure 3 lets you feel it. Cost (3) falls block by block. Time (2) is set by the slowest block, so a single block built from scratch wipes out most of the time saving. Try switching only the firmware back to scratch.

5How many ideas a year

Sections 3 and 4 lower two different numbers: the days d one test cycle takes, and the cost c of putting one idea in front of users. Let B be a yearly budget, k the number of parallel tracks a team can run, and D = 300 the working days in a year. The number of ideas a team can test in a year is

N = min(⌊B / c⌋, ⌊kD / d⌋)
(4)

The first term is the money limit and the second is the time limit. A team is bound by whichever is smaller, and only lowering that one helps: a cheaper idea does nothing for a team that is short of time.

5.1Hits

Most ideas fail. If users want each idea independently with probability p, the number of hits H satisfies

E[H] = Np
(5)
P(H ≥ 1) = 1 − (1 − p)^N
(6)
Proposition 2 (Number of tries) C2. Under Shenzhen-like parameters N is in the hundreds. Under remote parameters it is in the tens. At p = 2%, a remote team has about a one-in-three chance of a single hit in a year, while a Shenzhen team almost certainly finds several.
Proof sketch. With the values in Table 3, (4) gives N = min(375, 360) = 360 for the Shenzhen team and N = min(25, 20) = 20 for the remote team. Then (6) gives 1 − 0.98²⁰ ≈ 0.33 and 1 − 0.98³⁶⁰ ≈ 0.999, and (5) gives expected hits of 0.4 and 7.2. ∎

5.2What speed cannot buy

Huang's book is about making things work and making them in quantity. That is where the round trip pays off. Each iteration answers a technical question (does it work?) or a production question (can it be made consistently?). The third question, whether anyone wants it, isn't answered on the bench.

Proposition 3 (Users settle demand) C3. Iteration lowers technical and production uncertainty. Demand uncertainty falls only when ideas reach users. A cluster speeds up innovation because it makes ideas cheap enough to put in front of users many times, not because any single build is fast.
Remark 1. Faster manufacturing doesn't guarantee market success. It lowers the price of a question. You still have to ask good questions, and listen to the answers.

6Two loops

Two loops run at once. The first belongs to the product team: design, prototype, assemble, test, find problems, revise. The second belongs to the city. Each problem one team solves teaches a supplier something. A mould shop that has fixed fifty enclosure rattles fixes the fifty-first faster, and over time suppliers specialise. Marshall described the same effect in localised industries more than a century ago [3]:

"The mysteries of the trade become no mysteries; but are as it were in the air."— Marshall, Principles of Economics, IV.x

Porter later called such places clusters and argued that they raise productivity and the rate of innovation together [4]. A simple model of the city's loop lets each finished product iteration shorten the next cycle, down to a floor set by physics:

d₍ₙ₊₁₎ = max(d_min, ρ·dₙ), d₀ = 30, ρ = 0.86, d_min = 6
(7)

This is why so much design innovation comes out of Shenzhen. Its designers aren't cleverer. They get more tries, the city remembers what every earlier try taught it, and the two effects compound. A designer who has watched five hundred ideas meet real users develops different instincts from one who has watched twenty.

7Limitations

The model is deliberately small, and proximity can't shorten everything:

  • Tooling, certification and reliability testing take as long as they take. Steel moulds and drop tests don't speed up next door.
  • Complex, high-volume or high-quality products are limited by process capability, not distance.
  • Critical inputs such as leading-edge chips may come from distant suppliers anyway.
  • Concentration amplifies regional shocks. Inventory and second sources are the buffer.

It is also tempting to call Shenzhen the GitHub of hardware. Table 2 shows where the analogy holds and where it breaks.

Table 2. Software analogies and their limits.

The last row is the point of Figure 2. Software made the round trip almost free everywhere at once. Hardware can only make it cheap somewhere, and Shenzhen is where it got cheapest.

8Conclusion

Closer connections lower the cost of each test. More tests teach the cluster, and the cluster makes the next test cheaper. Read this way, Huang's Shenzhen isn't a cheaper factory but a cheaper question. That is why a team there can test hundreds of ideas a year while others test twenty.
